description: "Your AI agent just finished a job. It called a search tool, hit two APIs, wrote a file, ran a script, and asked a second model to review the output. Six tools, three services, one finished job.
Now prove what happened.
If you are lucky, one or two of those calls came with something checkable. The rest is the agent's word for it. That gap is the whole problem: agents do real work across real systems, and most of that work leaves no evidence behind.
Whole-job receipts close the gap. Every tool call in a job, whether Zambo executed it, watched it pass through a gateway, or your agent is reporting it, gets sealed into one checkable timeline. One link. Anyone with the link can verify the chain.
The design starts from an honest distinction. There are exactly three kinds of entries in a job timeline, and they are never mixed:
Executed entries. Zambo ran the tool itself. Zambo observed the request and the response, so Zambo vouches for the inputs and the outputs. These carry verifiable receipts.
Observed entries. Zambo's gateway watched this call pass between your agent and a third-party tool. The external tool is named. Zambo vouches that it observed these exact bytes in transit. It did not execute the call.
Logged entries. Your agent or a hook reports that something happened outside Zambo. Zambo did not run it, did not watch it, and does not claim otherwise. What Zambo vouches for is narrower and exactly stated: the record was sealed with a server timestamp and has not been tampered with since it was logged.
That wall matters more than any other part of the design. A receipt system that blurs "we ran it" with "we watched it" with "someone told us it ran" is worse than no receipts at all, because it launders hearsay into evidence. Whole-job receipts keep the three claims in separate, clearly labeled lanes.
Every entry in the timeline carries one of three provenance badges, written so a non-technical reader gets it:
EXECUTED BY ZAMBO, Zambo ran this tool and observed the request and response.
OBSERVED VIA GATEWAY, Zambo's proxy watched this call pass between your agent and a third-party tool. The external tool is named. Zambo observed the bytes in transit; it did not execute the call.
LOGGED BY AGENT, An agent or hook submitted this record. Zambo attests the record is unchanged since logging, not that the reported action occurred.
The rule is mechanical, not editorial: an entry wears exactly the badge for what happened. A logged entry must never wear an executed or observed badge, an observed entry must never wear an executed badge, and an executed entry must never wear a logged one. Badge assignment is enforced in the receipt logic, not left to copywriters.
Each entry in a session's journal links to the one before it with a hash, forming a chain back to a genesis entry. The receipt id is a hash over the entry's canonical bytes, including its provenance type, so a badge cannot be swapped after the fact without breaking the chain.
The session timeline page shows entries in chronological order with their badges, plus the chain's root hash and a validity indicator. Verification is recomputation: take the public entries, recompute the hashes, compare. No account needed, no special access. If a single byte changed after sealing, the chain says so.
Timestamps come from Zambo's server clock, not the reporting client. A client can claim anything about when something happened; the seal records when Zambo received it. Submissions are screened for key-like material, which is redacted before storage, so the journal stays safe to share.
This is worth stating with precision, because precision is the product:
For executed calls: Zambo vouches for the inputs it sent and the outputs it received.
For observed calls: Zambo vouches that it watched these exact bytes pass through the gateway, and names the external executor.
For logged calls: Zambo vouches for when the record arrived and that it is byte-identical since arrival.
What Zambo never vouches for: the truth of an agent's report. If your agent logs that it called a tool and the tool never ran, the receipt proves the log entry is untampered, not that the call happened. The system is honest about the boundary between evidence and attestation, and that honesty is what makes the evidence trustworthy.
Agents log non-Zambo actions through a dedicated logging tool: hashes of what happened, never raw payloads. Local hooks can auto-log tool calls a coding agent makes, with inputs hashed before they leave the machine. Gateway deployments observe third-party traffic in transit and log what they saw.
Journal writes are authenticated, and anonymous submissions are rejected. Journal writes are rate-limited to prevent abuse, so the journal cannot be used as an infinite write pipe.
Three reasons, in increasing order of importance.
Debugging. When a multi-step job goes wrong at step 14, the timeline shows exactly what ran, in order, with inputs and outputs for the executed calls. You stop guessing.
Accountability. When an agent acts on your behalf across systems you do not directly control, a sealed timeline is the difference between "the agent says it did it" and "here is the checkable record." Audits, incident reviews, and customer disputes all get simpler.
Trust in agents generally. The agent economy runs on delegation, and delegation without evidence does not scale. A standard where every job ends with a verifiable timeline is how agents graduate from impressive demos to infrastructure you can build a business on. That is what the AER-1 open draft is converging on: receipts as the unit of agent accountability.
One job, one timeline, every call accounted for, every claim labeled honestly. Executed where we ran it, observed where we watched it, logged where it was reported. Checkable by anyone with the link.
That is a whole-job receipt.
Zambo is the execution layer that gives AI agents real tools with a verifiable receipt for every call. Start free at zambo.dev. Read the open AER-1 receipt draft at zambo.dev/aer-1...."

### The Challenge of Verifying AI Agent Actions

As artificial intelligence (AI) systems evolve, the complexity of AI agents' operations increases. These agents often engage with multiple tools and services, creating intricate workflows that are challenging to trace. The verification of actions taken by AI agents is crucial, especially when these agents operate across diverse platforms. Without a clear and verifiable record of their actions, stakeholders may struggle to trust AI systems, particularly in high-stakes environments such as healthcare and finance, where accountability is paramount. For example, in healthcare, an AI agent responsible for diagnosing patients must provide a transparent record of its decision-making process to ensure compliance with medical standards and foster trust among patients and providers.

### The Accountability Gap in Multi-Tool AI Environments

In multi-tool environments, the lack of accountability can lead to significant issues. For instance, if an AI agent claims to have processed data through several APIs but cannot provide a verifiable record, stakeholders must rely solely on the agent's assertions. This reliance can lead to disputes, errors, and a general mistrust of AI systems. Consider a scenario where an AI agent is responsible for processing sensitive financial transactions. If an error occurs, the inability to trace the exact actions taken by the agent can result in regulatory penalties and loss of customer trust. For instance, a bank deploying an AI to assess loan applications might find itself facing legal challenges if it cannot demonstrate the basis for its decisions, emphasizing the need for a robust accountability framework.

### Introducing Whole-Job Receipts: A Solution for Accountability

Whole-job receipts offer a promising solution to the accountability gap in AI operations. These receipts provide a structured framework that documents every action taken by an AI agent in a single, verifiable timeline. By capturing all interactions—whether executed, observed, or logged—whole-job receipts allow stakeholders to trace the entire workflow of an AI job. This structured documentation not only enhances accountability but also fosters trust among users, as they can verify the actions taken by the agent through a transparent and immutable record. For example, in a customer service AI application, a whole-job receipt could document every interaction with users, from initial inquiries to final resolutions, allowing for thorough audits and performance evaluations.

### Understanding Whole-Job Receipts: Types of Entries Defined

Whole-job receipts consist of three distinct types of entries: executed, observed, and logged.

1. **Executed Entries**: These entries signify actions that the AI agent directly performed. For example, if an AI agent invokes a machine learning model for predictions, this action is recorded as an executed entry. The entry details the inputs provided and the outputs generated, ensuring complete traceability. In a recommendation system, an executed entry might detail the specific algorithm used and the user data considered in generating recommendations.

2. **Observed Entries**: These entries document actions that the AI agent did not perform but witnessed. For example, if the agent interacts with an external API through a gateway, the observed entry captures the bytes transmitted and received without claiming execution. This distinction is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the logged data. In a logistics AI, an observed entry could record the response from a shipping service when querying for delivery times, ensuring that the AI's decision-making process is transparent.

3. **Logged Entries**: These entries represent actions reported by the agent or a hook without verification of execution or observation. For instance, if an agent logs that it attempted to access a service, this entry is marked as logged, indicating that while the record is untampered, the actual execution of the call cannot be verified. In a data processing scenario, a logged entry might indicate that the AI attempted to load a dataset from a database, but the success or failure of that action remains unverified.

By maintaining these distinctions, whole-job receipts ensure that all claims made by the agent are clearly labeled and verifiable, thereby preventing the blurring of evidence.

### Provenance Badges: Enhancing Trust Through Clarity

Provenance badges are integral to the effectiveness of whole-job receipts. Each entry is accompanied by a badge that clearly indicates the nature of the action—whether it was executed, observed, or logged. This labeling system simplifies the verification process for users, allowing them to quickly ascertain the reliability of the information presented. For instance, a badge stating "EXECUTED BY AGENT" informs stakeholders that the entry is based on direct action, whereas "LOGGED BY AGENT" indicates a lack of verification. This clarity is essential for building trust in AI systems, as it provides users with a straightforward method to evaluate the reliability of the data. In practice, a clear provenance badge can be the difference between a user trusting an AI's recommendation or questioning its validity.

### Debugging and Accountability: Practical Implications of Whole-Job Receipts

The practical implications of whole-job receipts extend beyond accountability; they significantly enhance debugging capabilities. When an AI job encounters an error, the structured timeline allows developers to pinpoint the exact actions executed, including the inputs and outputs at each step. For example, if an AI agent fails to produce expected results after a series of transformations, developers can review the executed entries to identify discrepancies in the data flow. This level of detail reduces the time spent guessing what went wrong and facilitates a more efficient troubleshooting process. In a scenario where an AI model's predictions are consistently inaccurate, developers can analyze the executed entries to determine if the model was fed incorrect data, allowing for quicker resolution.

### Real-World Applications: Transforming AI Infrastructure

The implementation of whole-job receipts has far-reaching implications for AI infrastructure. In sectors such as finance, where compliance and audit trails are critical, the ability to provide verifiable timelines for AI operations can streamline regulatory processes. For instance, consider a financial institution deploying AI to analyze transactions for fraud detection. With whole-job receipts, the institution can demonstrate to regulators exactly how the AI made its decisions, providing a clear audit trail that enhances both accountability and compliance. This capability not only satisfies regulatory requirements but also reassures customers that their transactions are being handled with diligence.

Moreover, whole-job receipts can facilitate the integration of AI systems with existing infrastructures. By providing a standardized method for documenting actions, organizations can more easily incorporate AI agents into their workflows without sacrificing transparency or reliability. This standardization is particularly valuable in environments where multiple teams or departments rely on AI outputs for decision-making. For example, in a retail company, a whole-job receipt could document how an AI system determines inventory needs based on sales data, allowing various departments to align their strategies based on a shared understanding of the AI's operations.

### Challenges and Limitations of Whole-Job Receipts

Despite their advantages, the implementation of whole-job receipts is not without challenges. One significant hurdle is ensuring that all tools and services used by AI agents support the necessary logging and observation capabilities. Legacy systems or poorly designed APIs may not provide the required visibility, complicating the creation of comprehensive whole-job receipts. Additionally, the overhead associated with maintaining a detailed logging system could impact performance, particularly in high-frequency trading or real-time analytics scenarios where latency is critical. Organizations must weigh the benefits of enhanced accountability against the potential costs in system performance and resource allocation.

### Future Directions: Scaling Whole-Job Receipts in AI Systems

As the landscape of AI continues to evolve, the adoption of whole-job receipts is likely to expand. Future developments may include enhanced automation for generating receipts, incorporating machine learning techniques to analyze the logged data for anomalies, and improving integration with third-party tools. Furthermore, as AI systems become increasingly autonomous, the need for robust accountability mechanisms will only grow, making whole-job receipts a foundational element of future AI infrastructures. Organizations that prioritize this accountability will be better positioned to navigate the complexities of AI deployment and maintain stakeholder trust.

### Conclusion: Building a Trustworthy AI Ecosystem

Whole-job receipts represent a significant advancement in the quest for accountability and trust in AI systems. By providing a clear, verifiable record of actions taken by AI agents, these receipts not only enhance debugging capabilities but also foster trust among users and stakeholders. As organizations continue to integrate AI into their operations, the adoption of whole-job receipts will be crucial in establishing a trustworthy AI ecosystem that can support complex, multi-tool workflows while ensuring accountability and transparency. The future of AI infrastructure hinges on our ability to create systems that not only perform tasks but also provide the necessary transparency to validate their operations, ultimately fostering a more reliable and trusted AI landscape.
