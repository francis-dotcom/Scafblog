It lives inside delegated identity permissions granted through OAuth. These non-human identities (NHIs) are rapidly becoming one of the fastest-growing attack surfaces in enterprise environments.
Most organizations still aren’t monitoring them.
The explosion of AI productivity tools has changed how enterprise data leaves the organization. Employees no longer need to install software or provision infrastructure. They simply click “Sign in with Microsoft” or “Sign in with Google.”
Within seconds, a third-party application receives broad delegated access to corporate resources. The firewall sees nothing. Endpoint detection reports no anomalies. No malware runs. No suspicious connections appear.

The traditional perimeter hasn’t shifted — it has been quietly bypassed.

Figure 1: Modern AI SaaS integrations bypass traditional network controls by establishing persistent OAuth trust relationships. The refresh token — not the firewall — is now the critical security boundary.
OAuth was designed to solve a legitimate problem: users shouldn’t share passwords with every app. Instead, identity providers issue scoped tokens.
This model works well — until modern AI platforms demand deep access to deliver value. An AI assistant cannot summarize emails it cannot read, organize documents it cannot access, or automate workflows it cannot see.
As a result, many AI tools request broad permissions such as:
Mail.ReadWrite
Files.Read.All
Calendars.ReadWrite
offline_access (often the most dangerous)
The offline_access scope grants long-lived refresh tokens that survive password resets, MFA changes, and even delayed offboarding. These become persistent non-human identities — alongside service principals, workload identities, API keys, and automation accounts.
As AI adoption accelerates, the number of these identities is growing far faster than the number of employees.
What the employee sees: a helpful productivity tool.

What security teams should see: a persistent trust relationship with an external entity operating outside traditional governance.
An operations manager discovers an AI platform promising automated meeting summaries, document classification, and customer sentiment analysis. The website looks professional, reviews are strong, and the company appears legitimate.
She signs in with her corporate Microsoft account and grants the requested permissions.
Nothing malicious happens that day. The tool works as advertised.
But from that moment, part of the organization’s security posture now depends on the vendor’s security maturity. If the vendor is breached, leaks refresh tokens, or falls victim to a supply-chain attack, the blast radius can be severe.
No phishing. No endpoint compromise. No stolen credentials.

The attacker simply inherits already-granted trust.
This is not theoretical.
In 2025, UNC6395 threat actors compromised OAuth tokens tied to Drift (later acquired by Salesloft). Using legitimate access, they reached Salesforce environments across hundreds of organizations. The integrations were trusted. The tokens were valid. Traditional controls missed the abuse.
Recent data underscores the scale: ~87% of applications in the average enterprise are unmanaged, only ~21% sit behind SSO, and AI tool adoption surged 181% in 2025.
Most IAM strategies focus on human-to-system interactions with Conditional Access evaluating location, device compliance, and risk signals.
OAuth flips the model. After initial consent, interactions become system-to-system. An AI platform can make thousands of API calls at 3 AM from a foreign cloud IP and still appear legitimate.
This creates a dangerous blind spot: strong human identity controls paired with minimal visibility into delegated applications.
Security teams must treat delegated permissions and non-human identities as core parts of the attack surface.
1. Restrict End-User Consent  
Require administrative approval for apps requesting sensitive scopes (Files.Read.All, Mail.ReadWrite, offline_access, etc.). Evaluate vendor reputation, business need, and permissions before granting.
2. Enforce Least Privilege  
Challenge broad permissions. Push vendors for granular scopes, resource-specific access, and time-limited tokens.
3. Continuously Audit Active Tokens and Applications 
Maintain ongoing visibility into:
Active OAuth apps and non-human identities
Refresh token usage
API call patterns and volumes
Permission changes
Abnormal data access behavior
Monitor long-lived apps with the same rigor as privileged accounts.
4. Automate Revocation and Response  
Build playbooks for rapid token revocation, app disabling, and investigation. Manual processes don’t scale against fast-moving threats.

### The Evolving Landscape of OAuth in AI Integration

As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes an integral part of enterprise operations, the security implications of OAuth (Open Authorization) are increasingly significant. OAuth is designed to facilitate secure delegated access, allowing users to grant third-party applications limited access to their resources without sharing credentials. However, in the context of AI tools and non-human identities (NHIs), OAuth's implementation raises critical security concerns. The shift from traditional human-to-system interactions to system-to-system communications creates new vulnerabilities that organizations must address.

### The Surge of Non-Human Identities and Their Risks

Non-human identities, such as service accounts, API keys, and automation accounts, are proliferating as organizations adopt AI tools. These identities often operate under OAuth permissions, enabling automated systems to perform actions that were once the domain of human users. The rapid growth of NHIs presents a significant attack surface; in fact, recent statistics indicate that approximately 87% of applications within enterprises are unmanaged, with only about 21% protected by Single Sign-On (SSO) solutions. This lack of oversight means that NHIs can operate with minimal visibility, making them attractive targets for malicious actors.

### The Security Blind Spots of OAuth Permissions

The traditional security perimeter has shifted, and OAuth tokens have become a new boundary. Once granted, OAuth permissions allow applications to make numerous API calls autonomously, often without further human intervention. This creates a dangerous blind spot; if an AI tool is compromised, attackers can exploit existing OAuth tokens to gain access to sensitive data and systems. For example, if an organization integrates an AI tool that requests broad permissions such as `Mail.ReadWrite` or `Files.Read.All`, it opens itself up to significant risk if that tool is later compromised. The trust established during the initial consent phase can be leveraged by attackers to bypass traditional security measures.

### Case Study: The Drift Incident and Its Implications

A notable case that illustrates the risks associated with OAuth token management occurred in 2025 when threat actors exploited compromised OAuth tokens linked to the Drift application. This breach allowed attackers to access Salesforce environments across numerous organizations, demonstrating how a single compromised application can lead to widespread consequences. The tokens were valid, and traditional security controls failed to detect the unauthorized access. This incident underscores the need for organizations to adopt a proactive approach to managing OAuth permissions and monitoring NHIs.

### The Alarming Statistics of AI Tool Adoption

The rapid adoption of AI tools further complicates the security landscape. In 2025 alone, AI tool adoption surged by 181%, reflecting a significant shift toward reliance on third-party applications. This trend is concerning, given that many organizations lack adequate oversight of these applications. The combination of unmanaged applications and the increasing number of NHIs emphasizes the urgent need for organizations to reassess their security strategies, particularly in relation to OAuth permissions.

### Best Practices for Mitigating OAuth Risks

To effectively manage OAuth risks in AI-driven environments, organizations should implement several best practices. One effective strategy is to restrict end-user consent by requiring administrative approval for applications requesting sensitive scopes. This ensures that applications are thoroughly evaluated based on their reputation, business need, and requested permissions before access is granted. By implementing such controls, organizations can significantly reduce the risk of unauthorized access.

### Enforcing the Principle of Least Privilege

Another critical best practice is to enforce the principle of least privilege for AI applications. Organizations should challenge broad permissions and advocate for more granular scopes that limit access to only what is necessary for the application's functionality. For instance, instead of granting `offline_access`, which provides long-lived refresh tokens, organizations should push for time-limited tokens that expire after a set duration. This approach not only enhances security but also aligns with compliance requirements in regulated industries.

### Continuous Monitoring and Auditing of OAuth Tokens

Continuous monitoring and auditing of OAuth tokens and NHIs are essential for maintaining visibility over active permissions. Organizations should implement ongoing monitoring of token usage, API call patterns, and permission changes to identify abnormal data access behavior. For example, if an AI application begins making an unusually high volume of API calls during off-hours, this could indicate a potential security incident. By treating NHIs with the same rigor as privileged accounts, organizations can enhance their overall security posture.

### Automating Incident Response for OAuth-Related Breaches

Given the rapid pace of AI tool adoption and the evolving threat landscape, organizations must also automate their response strategies for OAuth-related security incidents. Manual processes for revoking tokens or disabling applications are often too slow to respond to fast-moving threats. Developing playbooks that outline rapid response actions, such as token revocation and application disabling, can significantly reduce the impact of a security breach. Automation not only improves response times but also ensures consistency in handling security incidents.

### The Future of Security in AI-Driven Environments

As AI continues to reshape enterprise environments, the security landscape must adapt accordingly. Organizations must recognize that the traditional perimeter has shifted; OAuth tokens have emerged as the new security boundary. Trust is no longer granted solely to individuals but also to software applications. This paradigm shift necessitates a reevaluation of security strategies, focusing on the management of delegated identities and OAuth permissions.

### Conclusion: Balancing Innovation and Security

In conclusion, while AI tools offer significant benefits in terms of productivity and efficiency, they also introduce new security challenges that cannot be ignored. Organizations must strike a balance between leveraging AI capabilities and maintaining robust security measures. By implementing best practices for managing OAuth risks, enforcing least privilege access, and continuously auditing NHIs, organizations can navigate the complexities of AI integration while safeguarding their critical assets. The journey toward securing AI-driven environments is ongoing, but with the right strategies in place, organizations can embrace innovation without compromising security.
