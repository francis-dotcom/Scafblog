An LLM gateway (same thing as an AI gateway or API router) is a proxy between your app and the providers. Your code calls the gateway instead of api.openai.com, and the gateway handles everything around the model call:
One OpenAI-compatible format for every provider, so switching models is config, not a rewrite.
Automatic fallback and retries when a provider has an outage or rate-limits you.
Centralized credentials: services get scoped virtual keys, the gateway holds the real ones.
Caching (exact or semantic) so duplicate prompts do not hit the bill twice.
Budgets, rate limits, and per-team cost attribution.
It is the load balancer argument, applied to model APIs. The pain that pushes teams there is always concrete: twelve services calling gpt-4o directly all time out together during one OpenAI incident, or a production Anthropic key ends up pasted into three repos because there was no virtual key system.

OpenRouter is the default answer when you want the widest catalog and zero infrastructure. There is no markup on per-token pricing; the business runs on a 5.5% credit top-up fee (minimum $0.80), 5% on crypto, and 5% on bring-your-own-key traffic past the first million free requests a month. It raised a $113M Series B in May 2026 led by CapitalG with Nvidia's NVentures participating, at roughly a $1.3B valuation, so it is not going anywhere soon.
Integration is a base URL swap on the official OpenAI SDK, with provider/model slugs:
from openai import OpenAI

client = OpenAI(
    base_url="https://openrouter.ai/api/v1",
    api_key="<OPENROUTER_API_KEY>",
)

completion = client.chat.completions.create(
    model="anthropic/claude-sonnet-4.5",
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": "Summarize the CAP theorem in two sentences."}],
)
print(completion.choices[0].message.content)

The tradeoff: it is a closed-source hosted proxy sitting between you and every model call.

LiteLLM is the opposite bet: fully open source (about 55,000 GitHub stars, built by YC W23 company BerriAI), unifying 100+ providers behind an OpenAI-compatible interface you run yourself. Use it as a Python SDK, or more commonly as a standalone proxy your whole team points at, which also gives Go and Node services the same unified HTTP interface. A config.yaml maps friendly model names to real deployments, and litellm --config config.yaml puts the proxy on port 4000.
One thing you should not skip if you self-host it: CVE-2026-42271, a CVSS 8.7 command-injection flaw in LiteLLM's MCP test endpoints, affected versions 1.74.2 through 1.83.6. Chained with a Starlette host-header bypass (CVE-2026-48710) it became unauthenticated remote code execution, and it landed in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog with active exploitation observed. Fixed in 1.83.7. Patch promptly and keep the admin and test endpoints off the public internet.
Enterprise pricing (SSO, RBAC, audit logs) is negotiated per deal, not published.
Portkey pairs a fast open-source gateway (~12,500 stars, 1,600+ models, 50+ built-in guardrails) with a hosted observability layer: semantic caching, load balancing, virtual keys, content guardrails. Self-hosting the OSS gateway is free with no log limits; the hosted ladder runs from a free 10k-logs Developer tier (3-day retention, explicitly not for production) to Production at $49/month for 100k logs and Enterprise for compliance-heavy deployments.
The 2026 caveat is ownership. Palo Alto Networks closed its acquisition of Portkey on May 29, 2026, positioning it as part of securing AI agents alongside its Protect AI and CyberArk buys. The OSS gateway still works today, but there is no public roadmap commitment yet, so treat a multi-year bet on it as something to monitor.

If you already run anything on Cloudflare, this is the five-minute option. It is free on every plan and covers analytics, logging, caching, rate limiting, and fallback across Workers AI, OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini and Replicate. The paid extras are Logpush ($0.05 per million records past 10M/month, requires Workers Paid) and the new Unified Billing, which puts third-party model spend on your Cloudflare invoice for a 5% fee. It is deliberately not the most feature-dense gateway; it is the one you turn on because you are already there.
Kong AI Gateway extends an existing Kong API-management deployment with semantic caching, prompt compression, PII sanitization and MCP/agent traffic governance; it makes sense mainly if Kong is already your gateway of record. TrueFoundry targets the same buyer with 250+ models, RBAC, budget controls and on-prem deployment, priced from a free tier through $499/month Pro and $2,999/month Pro Plus, with about $21M raised across Peak XV and Intel Capital rounds.
Four routing startups changed identity within roughly five months: Portkey (acquired), Helicone (acquired and moved to maintenance mode after processing 14+ trillion tokens for ~16,000 orgs), Martian (now an interpretability research company), and Not Diamond (narrowed to coding-agent routing). The category is not dying; it matured fast enough that platforms wanted to own it, and pure routing turned out not to be a defensible standalone business.
The practical filter: pick something you can fork and self-host (LiteLLM, Portkey's OSS gateway), or something hosted by a platform with an obvious reason to keep funding it (OpenRouter, Cloudflare). Be wary of small independents whose roadmap could change hands next quarter.
The fastest no-account path is LiteLLM: pip install litellm[proxy], export your provider keys as environment variables, write a config.yaml listing your models, start the proxy, and change only the base_url in your existing OpenAI client to http://localhost:4000. Then add a fallbacks list per model so a provider outage degrades gracefully instead of erroring at your users. The original guide walks through all seven steps with the exact config snippets.
Before you route anything, DevToolLab's AI Token Counter estimates token usage and cost across GPT-5, Claude, Gemini and Llama, and the cURL to Code Converter turns gateway curl examples into fetch, Axios or Python code.

### The Critical Infrastructure of LLM Gateways

As we move toward 2026, Large Language Model (LLM) gateways have evolved into indispensable infrastructure for AI applications. These gateways serve as intermediaries between applications and various AI model providers, facilitating seamless integration and management of diverse AI services. The growing complexity of AI systems necessitates a thorough understanding of the capabilities and limitations of these gateways for developers and organizations aiming to leverage AI technologies effectively.

### A Closer Look at Leading LLM Gateways

The LLM gateway landscape is dominated by several key players, each offering distinct features and pricing models. OpenRouter, LiteLLM, Portkey, and Cloudflare are at the forefront of this sector. OpenRouter provides access to over 400 models from more than 70 providers, making it a go-to option for those seeking variety without the burden of infrastructure management. In contrast, LiteLLM is an open-source solution that emphasizes self-hosting, allowing for greater customization. Portkey combines an open-source gateway with a hosted observability layer, while Cloudflare offers quick integration for existing users of its services. Each gateway addresses unique user needs, from ease of use to control and flexibility.

OpenRouter is particularly appealing for teams that prioritize a comprehensive catalog without the overhead of managing infrastructure. On the other hand, LiteLLM allows developers to self-host, offering customization and control over the deployment environment. Portkey's dual approach combines the benefits of self-hosting with the convenience of a hosted observability layer, while Cloudflare's integration is attractive for organizations already using its services.

### The Financial Implications of Pricing Models

The pricing structures of these gateways are critical factors that influence adoption and operational costs. OpenRouter's model, which imposes a 5.5% fee on top-ups, scales well for small to medium-sized applications but may become burdensome for high-volume users. LiteLLM, being free for self-hosted deployments, is ideal for organizations with the technical expertise to manage their infrastructure. Portkey's tiered pricing allows for scalability, but its recent acquisition by Palo Alto Networks raises questions about its long-term viability. Cloudflare's free offering is enticing for existing users but may lack advanced features compared to dedicated gateways.

### Self-Hosting vs. Hosted Solutions: Weighing the Options

When deciding between self-hosted and hosted solutions, organizations must consider the trade-offs between control and convenience. Self-hosting, as provided by LiteLLM and Portkey, offers developers the flexibility to customize their gateways according to specific requirements. However, this approach introduces challenges such as maintenance, security vulnerabilities, and the need for regular updates. For instance, LiteLLM experienced a critical vulnerability (CVE-2026-42271) that allowed for command injection, highlighting the risks associated with self-hosting. Organizations must implement rigorous security measures, including regular updates and vulnerability assessments, to mitigate these risks.

On the contrary, hosted solutions like OpenRouter and Cloudflare simplify deployment and maintenance but may limit customization. Organizations must evaluate their capacity for managing infrastructure against their need for flexibility and control. For example, a company with limited technical resources may prefer a hosted solution to mitigate the risks associated with self-hosting. Furthermore, hosted solutions typically offer built-in security measures, such as DDoS protection and regular security audits, which can provide peace of mind for organizations concerned about data breaches.

### Security Vulnerabilities: Understanding the Risks

Security is a paramount concern when deploying LLM gateways, especially given the sensitive nature of data processed by AI applications. The vulnerabilities associated with self-hosted solutions can be significant; for instance, LiteLLM's command-injection flaw demonstrates the potential for exploitation if security measures are not rigorously enforced. Organizations must adopt robust security practices, including regular updates, access controls, and monitoring for unusual activity.

For hosted solutions, while the responsibility for security primarily rests with the provider, organizations still need to ensure they are using secure API keys and managing access appropriately. Implementing scoped virtual keys is essential to minimize exposure in the event of a breach. For example, using a scoped key for each team can prevent unauthorized access to sensitive data. Additionally, organizations should conduct periodic security assessments and penetration testing to identify and rectify potential vulnerabilities in their AI infrastructure.

### Real-World Implementation: Use Cases in Action

To illustrate the practical implications of these gateways, consider a hypothetical organization, TechCorp, that aims to integrate multiple AI models into its customer support system. TechCorp evaluates OpenRouter for its extensive model catalog and ease of integration, allowing them to switch models without significant code changes. They leverage OpenRouter's caching capabilities to reduce costs associated with duplicate queries. This flexibility enables TechCorp to adapt quickly to changing business needs while maintaining operational efficiency.

Conversely, a startup named InnovateAI opts for LiteLLM to maintain control over its infrastructure and customize its gateway to fit specific requirements. By self-hosting LiteLLM, InnovateAI can implement unique caching strategies and model fallback mechanisms, ensuring high availability even during provider outages. This approach allows InnovateAI to optimize its AI resources while minimizing costs.

### Future Trends: The Evolution of LLM Gateways

Looking ahead, several trends are poised to shape the development of LLM gateways. The demand for more sophisticated AI applications will drive the need for gateways that offer enhanced features, such as advanced caching algorithms, improved load balancing, and better observability tools. Additionally, the integration of AI-driven analytics within these gateways could provide organizations with deeper insights into usage patterns and cost management.

As the regulatory landscape surrounding AI continues to evolve, LLM gateways will need to adapt to ensure compliance with data protection regulations. This may involve implementing more stringent access controls and audit capabilities, which will be critical for organizations operating in highly regulated industries. Furthermore, as AI technologies advance, gateways will need to incorporate machine learning techniques to optimize performance and security dynamically.

### Strategic Selection: Making an Informed Decision

In conclusion, the choice of an LLM gateway is a strategic decision that can significantly impact an organization's AI infrastructure. Developers and decision-makers must carefully evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of each option, considering factors such as pricing, security, and the ability to self-host. As LLM gateways become increasingly integral to AI applications, understanding their implications will be crucial for effectively leveraging AI technologies in 2026 and beyond.

By adopting a rigorous approach to selecting the right gateway, organizations can ensure they are well-equipped to navigate the complexities of AI infrastructure and drive innovation in their respective fields. The evolution of LLM gateways is not merely a technical shift; it represents a fundamental change in how organizations will interact with AI technologies in the coming years.
